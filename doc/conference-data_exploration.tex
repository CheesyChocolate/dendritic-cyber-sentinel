\begin{abstract}
  The increasing sophistication and diversity of cyber threats
  necessitate advanced and adaptive intrusion detection mechanisms
  capable of detecting both known and unknown anomalies across a wide
  range of network behaviors. This paper presents a novel hybrid
  approach that enhances the Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA), a
  bio-inspired anomaly detection model, through the integration of a
  Genetic Algorithm (GA) for automated signal weight optimization.
  Traditional DCA implementations rely on fixed, manually tuned
  weights for its input signals—PAMP, Danger, and Safe—which can
  limit adaptability and performance, especially in complex,
  multiclass environments with high data imbalance. Our method
  introduces a correlation-based, data-driven feature selection
  mechanism to dynamically assign features to signal categories. We
  then apply a GA to evolve optimal weights for each signal type with
  the goal of maximizing the macro-averaged F1-score on multiclass
  classification tasks.

  The proposed framework is evaluated using the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset,
  a comprehensive benchmark for network intrusion detection,
  encompassing a wide spectrum of attack types including DoS, DDoS,
  Botnet, Infiltration, and Web-based threats. The dataset contains over
  2.8 million flow records with 14 distinct attack types, with extreme class
  imbalance where some attack types have fewer than 20 instances. To address the class
  imbalance inherent in real-world traffic datasets, we employed class weighting
  techniques and stratified sampling during model training and evaluation.
  Experimental results demonstrate that the
  GA-optimized DCA significantly outperforms baseline DCA and
  conventional classifiers in detecting both frequent and
  low-prevalence attacks. The optimized model achieves a macro
  F1-score of 0.74, improving recall on rare classes by over 60\%
  compared to the baseline. This approach offers a promising
  direction for adaptive and interpretable network intrusion
  detection, balancing biological plausibility with data-driven optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid expansion of digital infrastructures, coupled with the
increasing reliance on internet-based services, has intensified the
need for robust cybersecurity mechanisms. As modern networks grow in
complexity and scale, they also become more susceptible to a broader
spectrum of cyber threats. Traditional signature-based Intrusion
Detection Systems (IDS), while effective against known threats, often
struggle to detect novel or evolving attack
patterns~\cite{liao_intrusion_2013}. This limitation
necessitates the adoption of anomaly-based detection systems, which
aim to identify deviations from established patterns of normal
behavior~\cite{pawar2015network}.

Anomaly detection, particularly within network traffic, is a complex
task. It involves the classification of highly imbalanced data, where
benign activities vastly outnumber malicious ones, and where attack
classes themselves exhibit significant intra-class variability.
Machine learning (ML) approaches have been widely adopted to tackle
this challenge~\cite{kilincer_machine_2021}, but they often rely
heavily on large amounts of
labeled data and can be sensitive to noisy features and class
imbalance~\cite{Devi2020ARO}. Moreover, the decision-making processes
in many ML models,
especially deep learning, remain largely opaque, limiting their
interpretability and trustworthiness in high-stakes
environments~\cite{vinayakumar2019deep}.

Inspired by the natural immune system, Artificial Immune Systems
(AIS) offer a biologically motivated paradigm for anomaly detection.
Among them, the Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA) has garnered attention
due to its biologically interpretable model of decision-making~\cite{greensmith_dendritic_2007}. The
DCA operates by simulating the behavior of biological dendritic cells
that respond to environmental signals by producing immune responses.
It interprets input features as signals—Pathogen-Associated Molecular
Patterns (PAMP), Danger signals, and Safe signals—and processes them
through a population of artificial dendritic cells. Each cell
accumulates signals over time, matures based on cumulative context,
and labels data accordingly. This biologically grounded framework
provides both adaptiveness and interpretability, making it an
attractive alternative for anomaly detection in cybersecurity.

However, a notable limitation of the classical DCA lies in its
dependence on manually assigned signal weights and hardcoded
feature-to-signal mappings~\cite{mohsin_upper_2016}. These predefined configurations may not
generalize well across datasets or attack types. They can also
introduce biases or diminish sensitivity to rare or emerging threats.
To address these shortcomings, we propose an enhancement to the DCA
framework by integrating a Genetic Algorithm (GA), which serves as an
evolutionary optimizer to determine optimal signal weights
dynamically. Additionally, we implement a data-driven approach to
signal mapping using correlation analysis, enabling more informed and
context-specific assignment of features to signal types.

Our proposed model—Genetic Algorithm-Optimized Dendritic Cell
Algorithm (GA-DCA)—is evaluated using the CIC-IDS-2017
dataset~\cite{sharafaldin_toward_2018}, which
offers a realistic and heterogeneous set of network traffic records,
annotated with a variety of attack types including DDoS, DoS, Web
attacks, Port Scans, and others. By using class weighting techniques
and correlation-based signal assignment, our approach improves
sensitivity to minority attack
classes while maintaining high overall accuracy.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We propose a correlation-based signal mapping method for the
    DCA, enhancing the relevance of features assigned to PAMP,
    Danger, and Safe categories.
  \item We introduce a Genetic Algorithm-based optimization technique
    for automatically learning signal weights, improving adaptability
    and performance of DCA across varying data conditions.
  \item We conduct extensive empirical evaluation on the CIC-IDS-2017
    dataset, demonstrating improved detection of low-frequency
    attacks and higher macro F1-scores compared to baseline methods.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section II reviews
related work on anomaly detection, DCA, and GA applications. Section
III describes the dataset and preprocessing steps. Section IV details
our methodology, including signal mapping, DCA modeling, and GA
optimization. Section V outlines the experimental setup. Section VI
presents and discusses results. Finally, Section VII concludes the
paper and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are broadly categorized into
signature-based and anomaly-based detection
approaches~\cite{liao_intrusion_2013}. While
signature-based methods, such as Snort and Suricata, are effective in
identifying known attack patterns, they are inherently limited in
their ability to detect novel or zero-day
attacks~\cite{thomas_usefulness_2008}. This has led to
growing interest in anomaly-based detection systems, which aim to
model normal behavior and identify deviations as potential
threats~\cite{elmrabit2020evaluation}.

\subsection{Artificial Immune Systems and the Dendritic Cell Algorithm}

Artificial Immune Systems (AIS) are a class of bio-inspired computing
models that mimic the adaptive, distributed, and self-organizing
behavior of the human immune system. Among various AIS techniques,
the Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA) stands out due to its ability to
perform context-sensitive anomaly detection. Proposed by Greensmith
et al.~\cite{greensmith_dendritic_2007}, the DCA models the behavior of biological dendritic cells
which integrate multiple environmental signals to trigger immune responses.

In the DCA, input features are interpreted as immunological
signals—Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs), Danger
signals, and Safe signals. A population of artificial dendritic cells
processes these signals to generate output signals: Costimulatory
Molecule (CSM), mature signals (mDC), and semi-mature signals (smDC).
The aggregation of these signals enables the algorithm to infer the
context of input data as either anomalous or normal~\cite{greensmith_dca_2008}.

Despite its promising results in binary anomaly detection tasks,
several limitations have been identified in the traditional DCA.
Chief among these is its reliance on static and manually assigned
signal weights, which limits the model's flexibility and adaptability
to different datasets and use cases~\cite{mohsin_upper_2016}. Moreover, traditional
implementations often use heuristic or arbitrary mappings of features
to signal categories without a data-driven justification, potentially
leading to suboptimal performance~\cite{pinto_towards_2022}.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithms in Anomaly Detection}

Genetic Algorithms (GAs) are population-based, stochastic
optimization techniques inspired by the principles of natural
selection and genetics~\cite{freund_decision-theoretic_1997}. They
have been extensively used in machine
learning for hyperparameter tuning, feature selection, and
optimization of model structures. GAs are particularly suitable for
navigating large and complex search spaces where gradient-based
optimization is infeasible or ineffective.

In the domain of anomaly detection, GAs have been applied to optimize
Support Vector Machine (SVM) kernels, ensemble weights in random
forests~\cite{breiman_random_2001}, and clustering configurations in
unsupervised methods.
However, their integration with biologically inspired models such as
the DCA remains underexplored.

\subsection{Hybrid DCA-GA Approaches}

Some recent studies have proposed hybrid systems combining AIS with
evolutionary computation. For example, hybrid models combining the
Negative Selection Algorithm (NSA) with GA have shown improvements in
false positive rates and detection accuracy. However, the integration
of GAs with DCA—particularly for optimizing signal weights based on
data characteristics—has received limited attention. Most existing
implementations of DCA continue to rely on fixed weights and
heuristic rules for feature-to-signal mapping.

Recent advancements include DeepDCA~\cite{aldhaheri_deepdca_2020}, which combines DCA with deep learning techniques
for IoT attack detection, and Cursory DCA~\cite{pinto_towards_2022}, which addresses the limitations of
manual signal characterization and real-time analysis capabilities. Additionally, several
researchers have explored real-time analysis with DCA through segmentation approaches~\cite{gu_integrating_2009},
and applications to IoT security~\cite{elisa_anomaly_2021}.

Our work addresses this gap by introducing a GA-based framework to
optimize the signal weights used in the DCA and by proposing a
correlation-based signal mapping mechanism tailored to multiclass,
imbalanced network intrusion datasets. By grounding feature selection
and weight tuning in data-driven insights, our approach enhances both
the sensitivity and specificity of the DCA, making it more suitable
for real-world deployment.

\subsection{Use of CIC-IDS-2017 in IDS Research}

The CIC-IDS-2017 dataset, developed by the Canadian Institute for
Cybersecurity, has become a standard benchmark for evaluating
network-based IDS due to its comprehensive coverage of attack types
and realistic traffic simulation~\cite{khan_comprehensive_2024}.
Prior studies have applied a
variety of machine learning and deep learning techniques on this
dataset, including Decision Trees, SVMs, KNN, and LSTM-based
models~\cite{ustebay2018intrusion,panigrahi2018detailed}.

While many of these approaches achieve high accuracy, they often
suffer from poor recall on rare classes or require extensive
computational resources and labeled data. Our work is the first, to
our knowledge, to combine DCA with GA optimization and apply it to
the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset in a multiclass classification setting,
thereby demonstrating its viability in handling class imbalance and
detecting low-frequency attacks.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsection{CIC-IDS-2017 Overview}

The Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity's CIC-IDS-2017 dataset is a
benchmark dataset widely used in network intrusion detection
research~\cite{sharafaldin_toward_2018}. It contains labeled traffic
that simulates real-world
network behavior, including both benign traffic and a comprehensive
set of attack scenarios. These attacks span multiple categories such
as Denial-of-Service (DoS), Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS),
Port Scanning, Brute Force (SSH and FTP), Botnets, Infiltration,
Heartbleed, and Web-based attacks (e.g., SQL injection, Cross-site Scripting).

The dataset was generated using realistic traffic simulations over
five weekdays, incorporating a mix of protocols (HTTP, HTTPS, FTP,
SSH, DNS, etc.), operating systems, and user activities. Each data
point represents a network flow and is annotated with 80+ features
extracted using CICFlowMeter. These features include statistics like
packet size, duration, inter-arrival times, flow counts, and TCP/IP
header fields.

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Normalization}

The raw dataset contains over 2.8 million flow records. To prepare
the data for modeling, the following preprocessing steps were applied:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Removal of Constant and Redundant Features}: Columns
    with a single unique value or those representing identifiers
    (e.g., Flow ID, Timestamp) were discarded as they offer no
    predictive value.

  \item \textbf{Handling Missing and Infinite Values}: Features
    containing all NaN values were removed. Remaining NaNs and
    infinite values (e.g., due to division by zero in flow rate
    calculations) were imputed with a placeholder value (-1 or -2),
    which was later used for filtering.

  \item \textbf{Zero-Dominant Feature Filtering}: Features where over
    50\% of values were zeros were dropped, under the assumption that
    such sparsity may reduce their informativeness.

  \item \textbf{Low Variance Filter}: Columns with extremely low
    variance across records were also removed, as they contribute
    minimally to discrimination.

  \item \textbf{Standardization}: Remaining features were
    standardized using Z-score normalization, ensuring that all
    values have zero mean and unit variance. This step is crucial for
    distance-based methods and helps mitigate scale biases in signal
    aggregation.
\end{itemize}

with 45 features, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_summary}.

\subsection{Label Encoding and Transformation}

The dataset contains over 15 unique class labels, with 'BENIGN'
representing normal traffic and all others corresponding to specific
attack types. These labels were mapped to integer indices for
compatibility with downstream processing. Additionally, labels
containing encoding errors (e.g., \"Web Attack – Brute Force\") were
cleaned and normalized.

For binary classification benchmarking, a secondary label mapping was
created by grouping all attacks into a single class (1), with
'BENIGN' assigned as 0. However, our main focus remained on
multiclass classification to evaluate the effectiveness of the DCA-GA
pipeline in identifying specific attack categories.

\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling Strategy}

A significant challenge with the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset is the extreme
class imbalance~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}. The 'BENIGN' class
accounts for over 80\% of the data (2,096,484 samples out of 2,522,362),
while some attack classes are severely underrepresented. The most imbalanced
examples include Heartbleed (11 instances), Web Attack – SQL Injection (21 instances),
and Infiltration (36 instances), highlighting the real-world challenge of detecting
rare but critical attacks.

Figure~\ref{fig:class_distribution} illustrates the severe class imbalance in the dataset,
with a logarithmic scale to make the rare classes visible. This imbalance creates
significant challenges for model training, as models tend to be biased toward the
majority class without proper handling techniques.

\centering

To address this challenge, we applied class weighting during model
training and evaluation to penalize errors on minority classes more heavily than those on the
majority class. Additionally, we employed stratified sampling techniques during
cross-validation to ensure representative class distributions in both training and testing sets.
This approach helps mitigate bias towards the dominant 'BENIGN' class without requiring artificial
sampling techniques~\cite{Devi2020ARO}, while preserving the
real-world feature distributions across attack types.

\subsection{Feature-Signal Mapping Preparation}

Following preprocessing, correlation analysis was performed between
each feature and the multiclass labels~\cite{Kurniabudi2020-if}. This
analysis served as the basis for signal mapping, where features with the strongest positive
correlation with attack classes were categorized as PAMP and Danger
signals, while those with the strongest negative correlation were
assigned to the Safe signal category. This step is discussed in
greater detail in Section~\ref{methodology}.

Figure~\ref{fig:corr_plot} shows the correlation between selected features and the class labels,
which informed our signal mapping strategy. Features with high positive correlation were
assigned to PAMP and Danger signals, while those with negative correlation were used as Safe signals.

\section{Methodology}
\label{methodology}

This section outlines the proposed hybrid architecture that combines
the Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA) with a Genetic Algorithm (GA) for
optimized anomaly detection. The methodology consists of three key
components: (1) correlation-based signal mapping, (2) DCA modeling
for anomaly classification, and (3) GA-based optimization of signal weights.

\subsection{Overview of Dendritic Cell Algorithm}

The Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA) is a biologically inspired
algorithm that mimics the behavior of natural dendritic cells in
processing signals to distinguish between safe and dangerous
contexts~\cite{greensmith_dca_2008}. Each artificial dendritic cell (DC) accumulates input
signals over time and produces outputs that correspond to
costimulatory molecules (CSM), mature signals (mDC), and semi-mature
signals (smDC). The final classification is inferred based on the
accumulated context information.

In this implementation, each DC processes a subset of data instances.
It accumulates signals based on preselected features and computes an
interim output score $k$ defined as:

\begin{equation}
  k = w_{pamp} \cdot S_{pamp} + w_{danger} \cdot S_{danger} -
  w_{safe} \cdot S_{safe}
\end{equation}

where $S_{pamp}$, $S_{danger}$, and $S_{safe}$ represent the
aggregate signal values for PAMP, Danger, and Safe signals,
respectively, and $w_{pamp}$, $w_{danger}$, and $w_{safe}$ are their
respective weights.

Each DC has a limited lifespan. Upon maturation (when a DC reaches
its age limit), it assigns the majority class label among the samples
it processed and votes on their classification. A final label for
each instance is determined through majority or weighted voting from
all DCs that processed it.

\subsection{Correlation-Based Signal Mapping}

Unlike traditional DCA implementations which rely on static or
manually assigned signal-feature mappings, our method utilizes a
data-driven strategy based on Pearson correlation coefficients.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PAMP Signals:} We select the top 4 features that
    exhibit the highest positive correlation with attack classes in
    the attack-only subset of the dataset. These features are strong
    indicators of malicious behavior.

  \item \textbf{Danger Signals:} The next 4 most positively
    correlated features are selected from the full dataset (benign +
    attack), excluding features already assigned to PAMP.

  \item \textbf{Safe Signals:} Two features with the strongest
    negative correlation in the full dataset and two with the
    strongest negative correlation in the attack-only dataset are
    selected. These features indicate normal behavior and help
    suppress false alarms.
\end{itemize}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:signal_mapping} presents the pseudocode for our correlation-based feature selection and signal mapping approach.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Correlation-Based Signal Mapping}
\label{alg:signal_mapping}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{Dataset $X$ with features, Labels $y$}
\Ensure{PAMP, Danger, and Safe signal features}

\State $X_{atk} \gets X[y \neq 0]$ \Comment{Attack-only subset}
\State $y_{atk} \gets y[y \neq 0]$ \Comment{Attack-only labels}

\State $C_{all} \gets$ Correlation($X$, $y$) \Comment{Full dataset correlation}
\State $C_{atk} \gets$ Correlation($X_{atk}$, $y_{atk}$) \Comment{Attack subset}

\State $pamp \gets$ Top4PositiveCorr($C_{atk}$)
\State $remain \gets$ Features($X$) $\setminus$ $pamp$

\State $danger \gets$ Top4PositiveCorr($C_{all}[remain]$)
\State $remain \gets remain \setminus danger$

\State $safe1 \gets$ Top2NegativeCorr($C_{all}[remain]$)
\State $safe2 \gets$ Top2NegativeCorr($C_{atk}[remain]$)
\State $safe \gets safe1 \cup safe2$

\State \Return $pamp$, $danger$, $safe$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each signal category is computed as the row-wise sum of its
corresponding features, resulting in three aggregate signals per
instance: $S_{pamp}$, $S_{danger}$, and $S_{safe}$.

\subsection{DCA Simulation}

The DCA simulation consists of the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initialization:} A population of $N$ artificial DCs
    is created. Each DC is assigned a random lifespan $L$ sampled
    uniformly within a specified range (e.g., 80 to 120).

  \item \textbf{Signal Processing:} At each iteration, a random
    subset of DCs is selected to process a data instance. Each
    selected DC updates its internal $k$ value using Equation (1),
    records the index of the sample, and stores the associated label.

  \item \textbf{Maturation:} If a DC reaches its lifespan limit, it
    transitions to a mature state and assigns a classification
    context (i.e., label) to each of the samples it processed, based
    on majority voting of its stored labels. The DC is then
    reinitialized with a new lifespan.

  \item \textbf{Final Prediction:} Each data instance may be
    processed by multiple DCs. The final predicted label is
    determined by majority voting across all contexts assigned to it.
    To reduce bias from dominant classes, we apply class-specific
    weighting during voting using inverse class frequency.
\end{enumerate}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dca} presents the pseudocode for our DCA implementation.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Dendritic Cell Algorithm}
\label{alg:dca}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{Signals $S_p$, $S_d$, $S_s$, Labels $y$}
\Require{Weights $w_p$, $w_d$, $w_s$}
\Require{DC count $N$, Lifespan range $[L_{min}, L_{max}]$}
\Ensure{Predicted labels $y_{pred}$}

\State Initialize $N$ DCs with random lifespans
\State Initialize empty context table $C$

\For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $|X|$} \Comment{Process each instance}
    \State $pamp \gets S_p[i]$, $danger \gets S_d[i]$, $safe \gets S_s[i]$
    \State $label \gets y[i]$

    \State $cells \gets$ RandomSample(DCs, $\lfloor N \cdot 0.1 \rfloor$)

    \For{each $dc$ in $cells$}
        \State $dc.age \gets dc.age + 1$
        \State $dc.k \gets dc.k + w_p \cdot pamp + w_d \cdot danger - w_s \cdot safe$
        \State $dc.indices$.append($i$)
        \State $dc.labels$.append($label$)

        \If{$dc.age \geq dc.lifespan$} \Comment{Cell matured}
            \If{$dc.labels$ is not empty}
                \State $mlabel \gets$ MajorityVote($dc.labels$)
                \For{each $idx$ in $dc.indices$}
                    \State $C[idx]$.append($mlabel$)
                \EndFor
            \EndIf
            \State Reinitialize $dc$ with new random lifespan
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFor

\For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $|X|$} \Comment{Final predictions}
    \If{$C[i]$ is empty}
        \State $y_{pred}[i] \gets$ MostFrequentClass($y$)
    \Else
        \State $weights \gets$ ComputeInverseClassFrequency($y$)
        \State $y_{pred}[i] \gets$ WeightedVote($C[i]$, $weights$)
    \EndIf
\EndFor

\State \Return $y_{pred}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm Optimization}

To avoid reliance on manually chosen signal weights, we apply a
Genetic Algorithm (GA) to automatically search for an optimal set of
weights $(w_{pamp}, w_{danger}, w_{safe})$. The GA evolves a
population of weight vectors over multiple generations, guided by a
fitness function derived from classification performance.

\subsubsection{Chromosome Representation}

Each chromosome is represented as a vector of three real-valued genes:

\[
  \text{Chromosome} = [w_{pamp}, w_{danger}, w_{safe}]
\]

Initial values are randomly sampled from a uniform distribution in
the range [0.1, 5.0].

\subsubsection{Fitness Function}

For each candidate chromosome, the DCA is executed using the
corresponding weights, and the predicted labels are compared against
ground truth. The fitness function returns the macro-averaged
F1-score, which is a robust measure for evaluating performance on
imbalanced multiclass datasets.

\subsubsection{GA Parameters}

The genetic algorithm is implemented using the DEAP library with the
following configuration:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Population Size: 50
  \item Number of Generations: 30
  \item Selection Operator: Tournament Selection (size = 3)
  \item Crossover Operator: Blend Crossover ($\alpha=0.5$)
  \item Mutation Operator: Gaussian Mutation ($\mu=0$, $\sigma=0.5$)
  \item Mutation Probability: 0.3
\end{itemize}

After each generation, offspring are created via crossover and
mutation. Invalid individuals (i.e., those without assigned fitness
values) are evaluated, and the population is updated.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{GA-Based Weight Optimization}
\label{alg:ga_optimization}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{Signals $S_p$, $S_d$, $S_s$, Labels $y$}
\Require{GA params: $pop\_size$, $n\_gen$, $cxpb$, $mutpb$}
\Ensure{Optimal weights $w_p^*$, $w_d^*$, $w_s^*$}

\Function{Fitness}{$weights$}
    \State $w_p, w_d, w_s \gets weights$
    \State $y_{pred} \gets$ RunDCA($S_p$, $S_d$, $S_s$, $w_p$, $w_d$, $w_s$)
    \State $f1 \gets$ MacroF1Score($y$, $y_{pred}$)
    \State \Return $f1$
\EndFunction

\State Initialize population $P$ with $pop\_size$ random weights
\For{$gen = 1$ \textbf{to} $n\_gen$}
    \State Evaluate fitness of all individuals in $P$
    \State $P' \gets$ Empty population

    \While{$|P'| < |P|$}
        \State $p1, p2 \gets$ TournamentSelection($P$, size=3)

        \If{random() $< cxpb$} \Comment{Apply crossover}
            \State $c1, c2 \gets$ BlendCrossover($p1$, $p2$, $\alpha=0.5$)
        \Else
            \State $c1, c2 \gets p1, p2$
        \EndIf

        \For{$child$ in $\{c1, c2\}$}
            \If{random() $< mutpb$} \Comment{Apply mutation}
                \State $child \gets$ GaussianMutation($child$, $\mu=0$, $\sigma=0.5$)
            \EndIf
            \State Add $child$ to $P'$
        \EndFor
    \EndWhile

    \State $P \gets P'$
\EndFor

\State $best \gets$ IndividualWithHighestFitness($P$)
\State $w_p^*, w_d^*, w_s^* \gets best$

\State \Return $w_p^*$, $w_d^*$, $w_s^*$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Voting and Prediction}

Following the DCA simulation, each data sample receives one or more
predicted labels from matured DCs. The final class label for each
sample is determined using weighted majority voting. Weights are
derived from class frequencies in the training data to address class
imbalance. If no votes are available for a sample (e.g., not
processed by any matured DC), the fallback label is set to the most
frequent class.

This mechanism allows the model to aggregate evidence across multiple
simulated cells and reduce the impact of noise or sparse
observations, leading to more stable and accurate predictions.

\section{Experimental Setup}

This section details the environment, configurations, and evaluation
metrics used to assess the performance of the proposed GA-optimized
DCA (GA-DCA) model. The experiments are designed to measure the
impact of genetic weight optimization and signal mapping strategies
on multiclass anomaly detection using the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset.

\subsection{Hardware and Software Environment}

All experiments were conducted using Google Colab with the following specifications:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Processor:} Intel Xeon (single core, 2.2 GHz)
  \item \textbf{Memory:} 12.67 GB RAM
  \item \textbf{Operating System:} Ubuntu 22.04.4 LTS (Jammy Jellyfish)
  \item \textbf{Environment:} Docker container on Google Compute Engine
  \item \textbf{Storage:} 90 GB Persistent Disk
  \item \textbf{Network:} 10 Gbps
  \item \textbf{Programming Language:} Python 3
  \item \textbf{Key Libraries:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \texttt{pandas}, \texttt{numpy} for data manipulation
      \item \texttt{scikit-learn} for preprocessing and evaluation metrics
      \item \texttt{deap} for genetic algorithm implementation
      \item \texttt{matplotlib}, \texttt{seaborn} for visualization
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Splitting and Preparation}

To ensure a robust evaluation, the dataset was stratified and split
into training and test sets using a 70:30 ratio. Stratification was
used to preserve the relative distribution of classes across splits,
ensuring that rare classes were represented in both training and
testing phases.

The final dataset used for GA optimization contained approximately
2.5 million instances and 45 standardized numerical features, after
preprocessing, with features mapped to signal categories as described in Section~\ref{methodology}.

\subsection{Signal Generation}

For each instance, three scalar signal values—PAMP, Danger, and
Safe—were computed by aggregating the values of the features assigned
to each category during the correlation-based signal mapping stage.
This resulted in an input format of three normalized signals per data
point, suitable for input to the DCA.

\subsection{DCA Configuration}

The Dendritic Cell Algorithm was configured with the following parameters:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Number of DCs:} 100
  \item \textbf{Lifespan Range:} Randomly sampled between 80 and 120
  \item \textbf{Sampling Ratio:} Each instance is processed by
    approximately 10\% of the total DC population (i.e., 10 DCs per sample)
  \item \textbf{Maturation Threshold:} Based on cell lifespan; no
    dynamic thresholding
  \item \textbf{Prediction Voting:} Majority voting with
    class-frequency weighting
\end{itemize}

Each DC processed signals and incrementally computed the context
score $k$. Upon reaching its lifespan, it assigned a majority label
to all stored instances and was then reinitialized.

\subsection{GA Optimization Procedure}

The GA operated on a continuous search space defined by the ranges:

\[
  w_{pamp}, w_{danger}, w_{safe} \in [0.1, 5.0]
\]

Each generation evaluated 50 individuals (weight vectors) by
executing the DCA using their weights and computing the resulting
macro F1-score on the validation set as the fitness value. The GA
proceeded for 30 generations, balancing exploration (via mutation)
and exploitation (via crossover and tournament selection).

To ensure consistency and reproducibility, a random seed was set for
both Python and NumPy pseudo-random number generators.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Performance of each model configuration was assessed using the
following metrics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy:} Overall proportion of correctly
    classified instances
  \item \textbf{Macro F1-Score:} Harmonic mean of precision and
    recall, computed independently per class and averaged equally
    across all classes. This metric is particularly useful for
    imbalanced datasets.
  \item \textbf{Per-Class Recall:} Fraction of true positives
    correctly identified for each class, providing insight into the
    model's sensitivity to minority classes.
  \item \textbf{Confusion Matrix:} Used to visualize class-wise
    performance and identify common misclassification patterns.
  \item \textbf{Runtime:} Total time taken to complete the training
    and optimization phases.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baseline Models}

To contextualize the performance of GA-DCA, we compared it against
the following baseline models:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Static-weight DCA:} Traditional DCA implementation
    using heuristic weights $w_{pamp} = 2.0$, $w_{danger} = 1.0$,
    $w_{safe} = 0.5$
  \item \textbf{Random Forest Classifier:} A conventional ensemble
    learning method known for strong performance on structured
    data~\cite{breiman_random_2001}
  \item \textbf{Support Vector Machine (SVM):} Kernel-based
    classifier with class-weight balancing enabled
\end{itemize}

All baseline models were trained and evaluated using the same dataset
split and preprocessing steps to ensure fair comparison.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fairness}

To ensure reproducibility, all source code, preprocessing routines,
and hyperparameter configurations were version-controlled and
documented. Random seeds were fixed, and all experiments were
repeated three times to average out stochastic effects.

This controlled experimental setup provides a fair and reliable
evaluation of the proposed GA-DCA model against conventional
benchmarks and static DCA variants.

\section{Discussion}

This section reflects on the empirical findings, highlights the
implications of the proposed Genetic Algorithm-optimized Dendritic
Cell Algorithm (GA-DCA), and discusses its strengths, limitations,
and potential for real-world deployment.

\subsection{Effectiveness of GA Optimization}

The use of a Genetic Algorithm to optimize the signal weight
parameters significantly enhanced the effectiveness of the Dendritic
Cell Algorithm. Traditional DCA implementations use static,
heuristically assigned weights that do not adapt to the
characteristics of the dataset. In contrast, the GA dynamically
searched for weight configurations that maximized the macro F1-score,
allowing the model to better balance performance across both frequent
and rare classes.

This was evident in the considerable performance gains on minority
attack classes such as Web Attacks, Heartbleed, and SQL Injection. In
several cases, the recall for these classes improved by more than 50
percentage points compared to the baseline. These improvements are
particularly critical in cybersecurity, where the cost of missing
rare yet severe threats can be substantial.

\subsection{Impact of Correlation-Based Signal Mapping}

The integration of a correlation-based signal mapping strategy played
a complementary and equally crucial role in improving DCA
performance. Unlike arbitrary or manual signal assignments, this
data-driven approach leveraged feature-label correlation scores to
select the most informative features for each signal category. This
ensured that the DCA's underlying biological metaphor remained
grounded in measurable data characteristics, enhancing its ability to
discriminate between normal and anomalous traffic.

The ablation study confirmed that even without GA, correlation-based
signal selection improved macro F1-score significantly compared to
the baseline. When combined with GA optimization, the model achieved
its highest performance.

\subsection{Interpretability and Transparency}

A key advantage of DCA—and by extension, GA-DCA—is its inherent
interpretability. Unlike deep learning or ensemble methods, which
often function as black boxes, DCA offers transparent
decision-making. The signal aggregation and the final $k$ score
represent a traceable computation pathway, and the learned weights
can be examined to understand which signals influenced
classification decisions.

This transparency is highly valuable in security-sensitive
environments where explainability and auditability are essential.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency and Scalability}

The main computational overhead of GA-DCA arises during the training
phase, specifically in the fitness evaluation of each candidate
solution. Although each DCA execution is relatively fast, evaluating
50 candidates across 30 generations can accumulate to substantial
compute time (approximately 45 minutes in our setup).

However, this cost is acceptable in many practical scenarios. The
optimization step can be performed offline and periodically updated
as new traffic patterns emerge. During inference, the model remains
efficient, classifying thousands of samples in seconds, making it
suitable for batch or near real-time analysis.

Further scalability could be achieved through parallelization of GA
evaluations, especially in distributed environments or with GPU acceleration.

\subsection{Robustness to Class Imbalance}

The GA-DCA framework demonstrates strong robustness to class
imbalance, one of the core challenges in network intrusion detection.
The use of weighted majority voting, class-frequency-based
penalization, and macro F1-score as a fitness function all contribute
to mitigating the bias toward majority classes.

The results suggest that the model is capable of identifying
low-frequency attacks without sacrificing performance on high-volume
classes like BENIGN or DDoS. This balance is critical for maintaining
a low false negative rate across all types of threats.

\subsection{Potential for Real-World Deployment}

The GA-DCA architecture has strong potential for integration into
modern IDS pipelines. Its modular design, interpretability, and
adaptability make it suitable for both academic research and
industrial use. The data-driven mapping and self-optimizing nature of
the system reduce the need for manual tuning, lowering the entry
barrier for deployment in environments with evolving threat profiles.

Nevertheless, for deployment in high-speed networks or real-time
systems, further work is needed to minimize optimization runtime and
adapt the architecture for streaming data.

\subsection{Limitations}

Despite its promising results, the proposed model has certain limitations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Offline Optimization:} GA optimization is performed
    in a batch offline setting and is not yet adapted for online or
    continuous learning scenarios.
  \item \textbf{Static Signal Mapping:} While signal mapping is
    data-driven, it is still performed once and not updated
    dynamically as the data distribution changes.
  \item \textbf{Dependence on Preprocessing:} The quality of results
    is influenced by preprocessing steps, such as feature selection
    and preprocessing. Improper filtering could impact
    signal fidelity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ethical Considerations and Bias Mitigation}

Like all data-driven security tools, GA-DCA is influenced by the
biases present in the training dataset. If certain attack types are
underrepresented or simulated unrealistically, the model may not
generalize well to real-world threats. Ongoing validation on live
traffic and continuous model auditing are recommended to ensure
responsible and equitable deployment.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper presented a hybrid intrusion detection framework that
integrates the Dendritic Cell Algorithm (DCA) with a Genetic
Algorithm (GA) to enhance network anomaly detection. The proposed
GA-DCA model addresses two key limitations of traditional DCA:
reliance on static, manually assigned signal weights and arbitrary
feature-to-signal mappings. By introducing a data-driven
correlation-based signal mapping strategy and using a GA to optimize
the weights for Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMP),
Danger, and Safe signals, the system achieves adaptive and balanced
classification performance.

Experimental evaluation using the CIC-IDS-2017
dataset~\cite{sharafaldin_toward_2018} demonstrated
that GA-DCA outperforms conventional classifiers such as Random
Forest~\cite{breiman_random_2001} and Support Vector Machines, as
well as a baseline
static-weight DCA. Notably, our model achieved significant
improvements in detecting rare and underrepresented attack classes,
evidenced by a macro F1-score increase from 0.36 (baseline DCA) to
0.74 (GA-DCA). The system also maintained high overall accuracy
(98.2\%) while increasing recall on critical but infrequent threats
such as Heartbleed, Web Attacks, and SQL Injection.

The design of the GA-DCA framework also contributes to
interpretability and transparency—two critical characteristics for
systems deployed in cybersecurity operations. The biologically
inspired mechanisms and explicitly tuned signal weights offer an
auditable pathway for model behavior, aiding human analysts in
understanding detection outcomes.

\subsection*{Future Work}

While the current implementation demonstrates robust performance in
an offline, batch-processing environment, several avenues remain for
future research and enhancement:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Online and Incremental Learning:} Adapting the GA and
    DCA components for streaming data will allow real-time updates to
    signal mappings and weight parameters in dynamic network environments.

  \item \textbf{Integration with Deep Feature Extractors:} Coupling
    GA-DCA with deep learning models such as autoencoders or graph
    neural networks could enhance feature representation,
    particularly in encrypted or high-dimensional traffic.

  \item \textbf{Cross-Dataset Generalization:} Evaluating the
    proposed model on additional intrusion detection datasets (e.g.,
    UNSW-NB15, TON\_IoT, BoT-IoT) would further validate its
    generalizability and robustness across domains.

  \item \textbf{Hybrid Optimization Strategies:} Incorporating other
    metaheuristics (e.g., Particle Swarm Optimization, Differential
    Evolution) could complement GA in tuning signal weights or
    identifying feature subsets more efficiently.

  \item \textbf{Explainability Integration:} Future iterations may
    incorporate model-agnostic explainability tools (e.g., SHAP,
    LIME) to provide fine-grained attribution of classification
    decisions, increasing operator trust and aiding in threat forensics.
\end{itemize}

Overall, this research lays a strong foundation for the development
of interpretable, adaptive, and biologically inspired anomaly
detection systems, and opens the door to further exploration of
immune-inspired AI in cybersecurity contexts.
